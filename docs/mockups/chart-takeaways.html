Jobs and prices

Unemployment

4.2%Sep 2026

Share of the labor force without a job, seasonally adjusted.

Unemployment hit 14.8% in April 2020, the highest since records began in 1948, and was back under 4% within two years. It has held between 4.0% and 4.5% since mid-2024.

Data notes

Share of the labor force without a job, seasonally adjusted. People who are out of work and looking for it, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics’ monthly household survey, seasonally adjusted. People who have stopped looking are not counted, so the rate can fall even when few jobs are added. The survey was not collected in October 2025, so the line has a one-month gap.

Inflation

3.4%Aug 2026

Year-over-year change in consumer prices (CPI, all items).

After almost a decade under 3%, inflation peaked at 9.0% in June 2022, the highest since 1981. It has eased since, but it hasn’t gone below 2.3% and climbed back above 4% in May 2026.

Data notes

Year-over-year change in consumer prices (CPI, all items). The change in the Consumer Price Index for all urban consumers, all items, from the Bureau of Labor Statistics. It tracks a fixed basket of what households buy, with housing the largest part, so it can differ from any one household’s costs. Prices were not collected in October 2025, so that year-over-year figure does not exist.

Borrowing

Mortgage rate

7.40%Oct 8, 2026

Average 30-year fixed rate, weekly (Freddie Mac survey).

The rate fell to a record low of 2.65% in January 2021, then nearly tripled to 7.79% by October 2023, the highest since 2000. It is still above 7%.

Data notes

Average 30-year fixed rate, weekly (Freddie Mac survey). Freddie Mac’s weekly survey of the average 30-year fixed mortgage rate. It is an average, not the rate every borrower gets, and it is not adjusted for inflation. Freddie Mac changed how it collects the rate in November 2022, so comparisons across that line aren’t exact.

Federal deficit

−5.8%FY2025

Federal surplus (+) or deficit (−) as a share of GDP, by fiscal year.

The budget ran a surplus only in 1998–2001. Every year since 2020 the deficit has topped 5% of GDP, a level that before 2020 it reached only in 2009–2012.

Data notes

Federal surplus (+) or deficit (−) as a share of GDP, by fiscal year. Budget receipts minus outlays as a share of GDP, from the Office of Management and Budget. Bars are fiscal years, which run October to September and are named for the year they end. A negative bar is a deficit; a positive bar is a surplus. Values are updated as budget estimates are revised.

Rules these follow. At most two sentences, about 25–40 words. Say what the chart shows, never why (no causes, no “thanks to”, no “soared”). Anchor on peaks, records and turning points that stay true when new data arrives; leave the latest value to the readout. Name a president only as a fact about who signed or who was in office.
