U.S. Naturalizations: 2024
Office of Homeland Security Statistics (OHSS) annual flow reports describe the movement of people into different immigration statuses. This Naturalization flow report presents information obtained from naturalization applications on the characteristics and number of people who naturalized during a given fiscal year.
Naturalization
is the most common way for someone to become a citizen in the United States other than by birth.
The Immigration and Nationality Act (INA)
[1]
and
Title 8 of the Code of Federal Regulations
[2]
,
[3]
provide the baseline requirements for naturalization, while
U.S. Citizenship and Immigration Services (USCIS)
oversees the naturalization process and procedures and decides if an individual has met all the requirements to naturalize. The U.S. Constitution provides equal benefits, rights, and responsibilities to foreign-born U.S. citizens as it does to natural-born U.S. citizens—such as the ability to vote in public elections—with few rights excluded, e.g., only natural-born citizens may become President of the United States. This OHSS
2024 U.S. Naturalizations Annual Flow Report
includes the 2024 fiscal year totals and characteristics of adult naturalization applicants and newly naturalized citizens.
[4]

Accordion Content 1
Summary
U.S. naturalizations fell to 818,570 in 2024, down from 879,790 in 2023 (Figure 1).
[5]
Although that drop was a 16% decrease from 2022 (969,380), naturalizations remained 12% higher than the 10-year average for 2010-2019 (730,100) that preceded COVID-19 restrictions.
[6]
Filed applications for naturalization increased 20% from 827,610 in 2023 to 994,130 in 2024 (Figure 2). One-third of all newly naturalized citizens were born in the top 5 countries of origin: Mexico (107,670), India (49,700), the Philippines (41,200), the Dominican Republic (39,880), and Vietnam (34,240). Half the newly naturalized population lived in the top 4 states: California (149,800), Florida (93,240), New York (85,700), and Texas (80,110).

Trends and Characteristics of Persons Naturalizing
Historical Trend
Annual naturalization averages have grown nearly sevenfold from 1950 to 2024 (Figure 3).
1950s–1970s: 122,610
1980s: 205,490
1990s: 497,870
2000s: 682,210
2010s: 730,100
2020–2024: 821,970
The rate of annual naturalizations over time has gradually increased with population growth, but was 28% lower in 2024 than at its historical highs in 1996 (1,040,990) and 2008 (1,046,540). The decision to naturalize is affected by a wide variety of factors, including political dynamics during election years,
USCIS
fee increases, and proposed or enacted statutory changes.
Figure 3.
Persons Naturalized: Fiscal Years 1910 to 2024
Source: March 2025 Office of Homeland Security Statistics system of record based on data provided by USCIS.
USCIS is predominantly funded by application fees and was thus affected by the COVID-19 pandemic’s rapid onset in 2020. Policy changes triggered a budget shortfall after the U.S. Government suspended in-person services and significantly reduced global travel. The data show spending reductions affected all USCIS operations, including naturalizations, which dropped from 843,590 in 2019 to 628,250 in 2020. After application processing fully returned in 2021, naturalizations bounced back to average 870,400 per year for 2021-2024.
Region and Leading Countries of Birth
Through the early 1970s, naturalized individuals were predominately European. However, the INA of 1965 increased U.S. immigration from Asia, which surpassed Europe as the leading region of origin for newly naturalized citizens. Large populations of Indochinese refugee arrivals in the 1970s further contributed to increased numbers of Asian immigrant naturalizations, and Asia has remained the top region of origin for the past 50 years (Figure 4).

Naturalizations by nationals from all regions of birth decreased by at least 12% between 2022 and 2024 except for South America, which decreased only 5.8%. Naturalizations from Africa decreased the most at 25%, followed by Asia (19%) and Europe (18%).
The top 10 countries of origin accounted for 48% of newly naturalized citizens in 2024. Mexico was the leading country of birth (13%), followed by India (6.1%), the Philippines (5.0%), the Dominican Republic (4.9%), and Vietnam (4.2%) (Table 2). From 2022 to 2024, the largest reductions in new naturalizations came from those born in Mexico (21,210), India (16,260), Cuba (13,480), and the Philippines (12,210), while the largest increase came from those born in the Dominican Republic (5,350).

Leading States and Metropolitan Areas of Residence
In 2024, 71% of newly naturalized individuals lived in the top 10 states. The top three states held 40%, with California home to 18% (149,800), Florida home to 11% (93,240), and New York home to 11% (85,700) (Table 3). Each of the top ten states experienced drops in naturalization numbers between 2022 and 2024, with the largest decreases in California (32,200), Texas (26,010), and New York (18,350).

U.S. naturalizations per 100,000 citizens dropped from 290 in 2022 to 240 in 2024. Fourteen states had more naturalizations per capita than the U.S. total, with Georgia and Illinois matching the U.S. total rate. Thirty-seven states, the District of Columbia, and Puerto Rico were below the country-wide per capita rate with West Virginia having the lowest at 30 per 100,000. Conversely, New Jersey had the highest number of naturalizations per capita at 480 per 100,000 citizens (Figure 5).

Years in Immigrant Status
New citizens in 2024 spent a median of seven years in LPR status before naturalizing, down from a median eight years in LPR status from 2017 to 2019, which matches its pattern before 2016 (Table 8). Of those naturalized, citizens born in Africa and Asia spent the least number of years in LPR status (6 years), followed by Europe and South America (7 years), and North America and Oceania (9 years). Those who naturalized from Europe, North America, and Oceania spent less time in LPR status than in years prior to 2021.
